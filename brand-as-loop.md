# Brand as Loop

*Why the most effective brands keep going in circles.*

Your brand book is ninety pages. Every detail's been fussed over by a whole team. Every few years, the book gets an update. Today that basically means your brand book is sitting still while your brand hurtles through a real-time global ocean of semantic currents where a Reddit thread, a product review, a competitor's launch and a training dataset all continuously revise what your brand means without asking you first.

As intelligent platforms and surfaces expand, so does the gap between brand book and brand ocean—in other words, between the meaning of your brand that you control, and the meaning out in the world that you don't.

In a recent workshop on positioning in the agentic era, trend forecaster and Nemesis agency founder Emily Segal put a cybernetic loop at the center of how brand strategy can close that gap. The canonical example of a cybernetic loop is the thermostat on your wall. You set a temperature. A sensor reads the room. The device compares the reading to the setting, and the difference determines what happens next: heat on, heat off. Then it reads the room again. The thermostat is not clever. It holds no feeling of winter. What makes it work is the loop: a set point, a sensor, a comparison, an action, and a return to the sensor.

The British cybernetician Stafford Beer spent the 1960s turning businesses into loops. An operations researcher who once built a cybernetics department inside a steel company, he modeled the corporation on the human nervous system and arrived at what he called the viable system model: an organization survives to the degree that it senses, compares, acts and corrects at every level of itself, without stopping. He was writing it up as *Brain of the Firm* when a letter came from Chile.

In November 1971 Salvador Allende's recently elected socialist government asked Beer to apply the viable systems model to the entire Chilean national economy. What Beer and his team built, on almost no budget, was Project Cybersyn — a requisitioned telex network strung three thousand miles down the country over microwave links originally installed to track satellites, wiring the nationalized factories to two mainframes in Santiago so that a shortfall on a production line showed up at the center in days instead of months.

Cybersyn's big test came in October 1972, when the owners of the country's lorry fleets struck, attempting to starve the government into collapse. Thanks to its flexible loop structure, Cybersyn was turned from monitoring production to managing distribution in less than 24 hours, running two thousand telexes a day; with somewhere between ten and thirty per cent of the normal fleet moving, food and fuel supply held at pre-strike levels.

Allende's government fell in September 1973, when a vicious right-wing coup fueled by the American CIA installed General Augusto Pinochet, and Project Cybersyn fell with it. Allende took his own life. Beer survived and applied what he'd learned in Chile to his next book, *The Heart of Enterprise*.

Now let's consider how brands can benefit from loops. Within a brand there's a set point: the positioning. There's a sensor: the tracking study, fielded annually, read once. There's an actuator: the campaign, the refresh, the guidelines update. What's usually missing is a comparison — a defined, recurring moment when somebody reads the gap between what the brand intended and what the market actually did, and uses that gap to decide the next move. Without that moment of comparison, brands risk being driven, as Beer once described them, "with the driver's eyes fixed on the rear-view mirror," unable to effectively manage future uncertainty.

Brand maintenance used to run on a multi-year clock because sensing was expensive. For the first time it's theoretically possible to run a brand at the speed that the meaning is actually moving. The risk: cheap optimization makes it easy to build a very fast loop around the wrong set point, and to get extremely good, very quickly, at doing the wrong thing.

The objection to all of this is that a brand you constantly revise isn't a brand. That identity requires something fixed, and a system forever adjusting has nothing to be loyal to. But what the loop revises is the path. What it holds is the set point, and the set point is where identity lived the whole time; the guidelines were only ever its filing copy. Identity produces action, action gets interpreted, interpretation revises identity, and the thing that comes back around is still recognizably yours. A thermostat keeps a room at seventy degrees by never once holding still. So choose the temperature, then build the loop that keeps it.
