Brand as Dialogue

Want to connect more deeply with your audience? Let's talk.

Essay Markdown ↓

Each side of the dialogue is governed by its own constraints: the brand by its series bible, the audience by its psychology and environment.

Back in Don Draper's day, the mental model for how brands reach customers was broadcasting. You packaged up your message and blasted it at scale out towards your audience, hoping you would find them. Things couldn't be more different today. Some brands get it, many still act like it's 1962.

A brand today needs to communicate like a conversation partner, not a broadcaster. Today, audiences have more means than ever to talk back, and they expect brands to listen and respond. This is why, when I joined Wolff Olins four years ago to lead their verbal practice, I designed an approach to verbal identity according to a dialogue model.

The thesis is straightforward. Treat your brand-audience relationship as an ongoing dialogue, and design how you communicate according to many of the same principles that govern good dialogue in literature, film and TV. The dialogue model jailbreaks the practice of copywriting from marketing prison and reimagines it as the art of managing dialogue at scale: not from one character or person to another, but from one large group of people to another.

In writing a scene, three things have to be settled first: who is speaking, where they are, and what each party wants. Who is speaking has to resolve to a character rather than a company — a verbal signature consistent and distinct enough that a reader can identify it even without the logo. Where they are does more work than most brand language systems account for. The same sentence means different things depending on the setting: in an error message, a checkout flow, an earnings call, and a chat window where the thing replying is software. And what each party wants is the question brand strategy is built to answer.

With a dialogue model in place, the familiar failures stop being matters of taste and start being diagnosable. In dialogue writing, David Mamet's first red flag is exposition cosplaying as a conversation— characters telling each other things they both already know, purely for the viewer's benefit. You could file most brand boilerplate writing here. His second is third-party talk: when two characters discuss an absent third, the real conflict is happening somewhere else in the building. So keep your dialogue with your audiences focused on them, and what you've got to give them now. Then there's tone. Succession made corporate-speak a character trait, using vague jargon as a shield to hide feeling and dodge commitment, and it works because everyone watching recognizes the move. A brand that writes in jargon has made the same character choice. The character is a man in a quarter-zip who cannot say what he means.

Defining the tones your voice can own is the first step out of the red flag zone. Brave, refreshingly candid, surprisingly bold — these can narrow the field but they also can be maddeningly vague, which is why a writer holding three adjectives at eleven at night can still has no idea what to do with the brief in front of her. What closes that gap is specification. Mailchimp is the instructive case: a side project that grew into a company of more than a thousand people, with a published style guide defining the voice with contrast pairs that fine-tune each attribute, and a POV on everything from sentence length, to forbidden phrases, to what the brand does not joke about. That is the altitude at which a voice becomes usable by someone who was never in the room where it was made.

Here's where the dialogue model starts to break down, which is also where it gets really interesting. In fiction, one intelligence writes both sides — In "Hills Like White Elephants," Hemingway knows what the man wants and what the girl wants, and engineers the friction from above. A brand writes one line and never the reply. Neither party is a single consciousness, either: the brand is a committee that has been arguing since the last planning cycle, and the audience is a statistical distribution with a group chat. And a character in a scene is held there by the scene. An audience is held by nothing at all. It closes the tab, or meanders away from the laptop. The obstacle in brand dialogue is indifference, which no dramatist would accept as a scene partner.

Television solved half of this decades ago. A TV drama runs on a very human storytelling machine: the writers' room. Many people writing multiple characters, season after season, without the essence of each character getting lost (ideal state, I know). The foundation is the series bible — a document fixing who each character is, what they would never say, and what has already happened to them. It is a production constraint, and it exists so that distributed authorship doesn't produce a different person every episode. The verbal identity systems I build at Wolff Olins are series bibles.

And the room has new writers in it. Support agents, product surfaces, the model answering a customer at three in the morning — all of them producing brand language at a volume no human room could staff. This changes what the bible has to be. A voice document written to inspire a writer is a mood board, and it dissolves on contact with a system that needs rules it can run. A voice document that can be executed is a spec. The job now is one document that does both, which is another way of saying that brand language has become infrastructure: load-bearing, invisible when it works, and catastrophic when it isn't there.

Every brand already has a writers' room. It has always been staffed by whoever happened to be typing — the intern on social, the rep on the phone, and now a model that answers ten thousand people at once and never gets tired of the question. The bible is the only thing all three can read.

Brand as Loop

Why the most effective brands keep going in circles.

Essay Markdown ↓

The brand as a cybernetic loop: a set point (positioning), a sensor (tracking), an actuator (the campaign or refresh) — and the comparison step: a reading of the gap between aim and execution.

Your brand book is the least intelligent object in the building. Ninety pages, every detail written by committee, refreshed every four years. Today, your book sits still while your brand hurtles through a real-time global ocean of semantic currents where a Reddit thread, a product review, a competitor's launch and a training dataset all continuously revise what your name means to somebody without asking you first.

As intelligent platforms and surfaces expand, so does the gap between brand book and brand ocean, between the meaning of your brand that you control, and the meaning out in the world that you don't.

In a recent workshop on positioning in the agentic era, trend forecaster Emily Segal — founder of the agency Nemesis — put a cybernetic loop at the center of how brand strategy can close that gap. I've been chewing on it since.

A cybernetic loop is an old idea and a simple one. Norbert Wiener named the cybernetic field in 1948, taking the Greek word for steersman, and the canonical example of a cybernetic loop is the thermostat on your wall. You set a temperature. A sensor reads the room. The device compares the reading to the setting, and the difference determines what happens next: heat on, heat off. Then it reads the room again. The thermostat is not clever. It holds no feeling of winter. What makes it work is the loop: a set point, a sensor, a comparison, an action, and a return to the sensor. Intelligence in this account is a property of the whole circuit rather than any specific part.

Stafford Beer spent the 1960s turning firms into loops. A British operations researcher who once built a cybernetics department inside a steel company, he modeled the corporation on the human nervous system and arrived at what he called the viable system model: an organization survives to the degree that it senses, compares, acts and corrects at every level of itself, without stopping. He was writing it up as Brain of the Firm when a letter came from Chile.

In November 1971 Salvador Allende's socialist government asked him to apply the model to the entire Chilean national economy, and what he built in the following year, on almost no budget, was Project Cybersyn — a requisitioned telex network strung three thousand miles down the country over microwave links originally installed to track satellites, wiring the nationalized factories to two mainframes in Santiago so that a shortfall on a production line showed up at the center in days instead of months.

Cybersyn's big test came in October 1972, when the owners of the country's lorry fleets struck, attempting to starve the government into collapse. The operations room wasn't finished. The network was. Inside twenty-four hours it was turned from monitoring production to managing distribution, running two thousand telexes a day; with somewhere between ten and thirty per cent of the normal fleet moving, food and fuel supply held at pre-strike levels. The system had been designed for one job and performed another, because what had been built was not a monitoring product but a loop, and a loop can be aimed at a problem nobody planned on.

Allende's government fell in September 1973, when a vicious right-wing coup fueled by the CIA installed General Augusto Pinochet, and Project Cybersyn fell with it. Beer survived. He surfaced back in the UK having diagnosed where he'd collapsed two levels of his own model into one, and the planning principles he improvised in Santiago turned up seven years later as a chapter of The Heart of Enterprise. When Brain of the Firm was reissued in 1981 he left the original argument standing and appended the entire Chilean story as a new final section.

Now put a brand where Chile was. The parts are all there but often the wiring isn't. Within a brand there's a set point: the positioning. There's a sensor: the tracking study, fielded annually, read once. There's an actuator: the campaign, the refresh, the guidelines update. What's usually missing is a comparison— a defined, recurring moment when somebody reads the gap between what the brand intended and what the market actually did, and lets that gap decide the next move. Beer's description of the alternative was companies driven "with the driver's eyes fixed on the rear-view mirror." Run it against Ashby and the diagnosis gets harsher than slowness. The semantic ocean has infinite variety; a brand platform has the variety of a static document. An arrangement like that doesn't regulate anything. It decorates.

I should be straight that the loop I'm describing is mostly an ambition rather than a report — I have not yet seen a brand function actually running one, with a sensing layer wired to a defined comparison and a standing mandate to act on the difference inside a fiscal quarter. Pretending otherwise would be precisely the kind of claim a loop exists to catch.

The main reason the brand loop paradigm has turned urgent is that the machines now transacting with your brand are themselves loop functions. An AI agent takes an action, observes what came back, and uses the difference to choose the next action. The intelligence we give the model is substantially a property of the structure around it, the arrangement that lets it act repeatedly and read the result.

This cuts in two directions at once, and the double cut is the whole argument. Your brand is now being read by systems that revise their picture of you every time somebody asks, which is why the static brand book is outgunned. And the same collapse in the cost of perception that produced those systems is sitting there for you to use. Brand used to run on a multi-year clock because sensing was expensive.

For the first time it is possible to run a brand at the speed the meaning is actually moving.

A flag, though, from Ashby: cheap optimization makes it trivial to build a very fast loop around the wrong set point, and to get extremely good, very quickly, at doing the wrong thing.

The objection to all of this is that a brand in permanent correction isn't a brand — that identity requires something fixed, and a system forever adjusting has nothing to be loyal to. Right worry, wrong object. What the loop revises is the path. What it holds is the set point, and the set point is where identity lived the whole time; the guidelines were only ever its filing copy. Identity produces action, action gets interpreted, interpretation revises identity, and the thing that comes back around is still recognizably yours. A thermostat keeps a room at seventy degrees by never once holding still. Choose the temperature. Then build the loop that keeps it.

The Trust Engine

You see a machine. I see a promise keeper.

Essay Markdown ↓

Your brand system as a trust engine: every internal and external surface — from marketing to claims to AI shopping agents — delivering the brand's signature around a single core of trust.

Recent research from Edelman now tags eighty percent of people as trusting the brands they use — more than they trust business, media, government, or their own employer. The prize for winning the trust race is a new game: among people who use generative AI, 91 percent already shop with it, meaning that the next reader of your brand's trust signals is a piece of software that doesn't rely on emotions, memories or vibes to make a call on your trustability.

To get at how brands can earn trust in the AI era, let's first watch how brand trust often falls apart: rarely in scandal, usually in a seam. Let's say you are an insurance brand. Prospects love your mascot — the disarming charm, the plain talk. Then the hailstorm comes, the claim gets filed, and the claim gets denied by a process reflecting none of the brand personality that was first so welcoming. Mascot joy, meet claim pain. The customer isn't only disappointed in the outcome; she's disoriented by the discontinuity. Trust doesn't live in the mascot or in the policy. It lives in continuity.

The second trust death is louder: the brand that can't stop saying "trust us." Trust belongs to that class of things ruined by the mode of their own transmission, like whispering "this is intimate" into a megaphone. Hamlet's mother had the diagnosis: the brand doth protest too much. Nobody trustworthy has ever needed to tell you this often.

So what is the thing called trust that's so fragile? Strip the sentiment and trust has a promise on one side, a belief on the other. Trust is a belief in the fulfillment of a promise — which makes it a signal to the future, like design. A chair's design is a promise that it will hold you; you sit without testing it (most chairs anyway). You trust a brand because it gave you something valuable before, and you believe it will again. 

Route that through the agentic era. When an AI agent assembles a purchase decision, it skips the marketing and goes straight to the record: does the spec match the review, does the review match the complaint log, does anyone credible corroborate? Edelman's own first recommendation — move from SEO to GEO, concedes the premise. But if that's all a trust engine means — translating your signals into the agent's mother tongue — we've merely redecorated the marketing layer. The more valuable machine is the one that heals the fracture between promise and fulfillment that can so often split brand experience in two.

Here's the rub: "automated trust" sounds like a scam. We assume trust can't be manufactured, because we model it on human sincerity. But brand authenticity—that beloved trust driver—was always the result of an exact match between what was promised and what happened. Picture a brand agent that meets the customer at the claim, knowing the whole backstory, what the ads promised, it speaks in the voice that made her choose you, and it walks her from mascot joy through claim pain with a continuity no call center manages at 2 a.m. on the third transfer. The agent has no feeling to fake. What it has is the promise, kept at the exact moment where promises are so often broken.

That's the trust engine: fulfillment infrastructure, in the brand voice. Because trust is a signal to the future, and the future is the one thing you can't fake. The brands that own the next twenty years will make fewer promises and keep them a million times. Design the chair so it holds. Then let people sit.

The Art of the Prompt

How to direct creativity by giving up control

Essay Markdown ↓
1
Author
Artist / Brand builder / Human
2
Instruction
Certificate & diagram / Brand guidelines / Prompt
3
Actor
Trained drafters / Agency, freelancer / LLM
4
Outcome
Wall drawing / Brand expression / Output

Conceptual art, brand expression and AI agents run the same four-step process.

Instructions are brand's unglamorous foot soldiers. Here's why they matter more than ever.

Sol LeWitt made drawings he never drew himself. Beginning in 1968, he wrote instructions — a few lines of plain language about direction, color, and how the lines should meet — and other people executed them on gallery walls. The instructions were the art. Buy a LeWitt wall drawing today and you get a signed certificate and a diagram, along with the right to have the piece drawn by trained drafters. When the show closes, the wall gets painted over. The certificate survives, and the next museum draws it again.

Every CMO already owns something like a certificate, and it's called the brand guidelines. Nobody treats that document as the work. It's the thing you make so the work can happen. But look at what you're asking it to do. The guidelines go out to people you will never meet: the agency in a market you've never visited, the freelancer covering a parental leave, the hotel franchise operator writing a room card at eleven at night. None of them were in the room where the strategy was debated, and all of them are about to speak in your voice. The guideline has to carry the direction intact while leaving them room to write a sentence that fits their needs—because no guideline can ever custom fit-every use case. Pull the guidelines too tight and everything reads like a legal notice. Leave it too loose and you have eighteen brands. LeWitt spent a career on exactly this problem, and his answer was to be binding about what mattered and silent about what didn't. Two crews executing the same certificate produce two visibly different walls, and both are correct. That tolerance is what makes an instruction usable by hands other than your own.

An LLM prompt is a similar document, addressed to a much faster set of hands. Which means the skill the agentic era rewards is one brand people have been practicing for decades: writing an instruction precise enough to survive execution by someone who wasn't there. It also means the anxiety points at the wrong end of the process. Drafting is what got cheap — the eleventh version of the launch email, the deck rebuilt overnight by something that doesn't sleep. Deciding what the work should be, didn't. Write the certificate. Someone else has always held the brush.

Sandals & Beaches Resorts

ClientSandals & Beaches Resorts AgencyWolff Olins RoleVerbal Identity Lead ScopeBrand voice, messaging, nomenclature, website rewrite, training

The brand that built the all-inclusive category, now deeply steeped in Caribbean soul. 

Sandals Resorts built Caribbean hospitality from the ground up — the swim-up bar, the butler suite, the all-inclusive category as the world knows it. Four decades in, the business partnered with Wolff Olins to fully reimagine the brand for a new hospitality landscape and a new generation of travelers.

The brand's verbal identity was holding it back. The existing copy read like the category, not like Sandals. Stiff, superlative-heavy, generic luxury-speak that could have belonged to anyone, a holdover from past eras and assumptions around how hospitality brands could best convey value and connect with guests. My job was to find the voice beneath the polish and build a system that could carry it everywhere.

The brand ethos: Natural Vibrancy. The Caribbean's simple pleasures connect us to the joy of living.

The verbal identity was built around a creative platform — Natural Vibrancy — and a brand persona we called the Island Insider: a local who genuinely believes the Caribbean is the best place on earth, and can't wait to show you why.

The Island Insider — the persona that anchors every brand voice decision.

From that persona came three voice principles that governed how the brand speaks across every context.

Three voice principles. Simple enough to be used by a ballroom full of hotel operators. Specific enough to actually impact how they write.

The new voice reflected the naturally vibrant spirit of the Caribbean that has animated Sandals from the beginning — more intimate, carefree, and relaxed, like a warm breeze off the ocean.

Why tell people how great the Caribbean is, when you can use language to take them there? 

The system had to work at scale. Sandals operates eighteen resorts across eight islands, with hundreds of staff members producing guest-facing copy every day. We built a voice spectrum — from Exciting to Informing to Guiding to Delight — that gave writers a clear brief for every context, whether they were crafting an ad campaign or a welcome letter. Then we rewrote the entire website using the new voice, with before-and-after copy that showed what the shift actually looked like in practice.

The verbal identity also shaped nomenclature — the system governing how resorts, property areas, and experiences are named — and extended into hospitality: how staff greet guests, how cocktails are named, how the brand sounds in a phone call versus a room card. At one point, I flew to Jamaica to train a full ballroom of hotel operators in the new brand voice.

Voice in the wild, capturing the "let fun be easy" spirit of Sandals' family-focused sister brand, Beaches. 

The work became the foundation of a global campaign across TV, print, digital, social, and out-of-home. The new Sandals verbal identity provided the blueprint that every partner agency built from — a brand that had always been felt, finally had something to say.

Benefit Cosmetics

ClientBenefit Cosmetics AgencyWolff Olins RoleVerbal Identity Lead ScopeBrand voice, verbal identity, brand strategy, global rollout

The purpose behind it all: Ignite Joy — because beauty should lift you up, not bring you down.

Benefit Cosmetics never struggled to be loved. Nearly fifty years after fun-loving twins Jean and Jane Ford opened a tiny makeup shop in San Francisco's Mission District, their company was the number-one brow brand on earth, sold in more than fifty countries. People adored the products. They just couldn't have told you much about the brand behind them. Somewhere on the way from scrappy shop to global bestseller the thread snapped: Benefit's bestsellers no longer added up to a personality, and the wit stopped reflecting a point of view.

Wolff Olins partnered with Benefit's creative and executive leadership to find that thread again. My job was to take that thread and spin it into a voice that Benefit practitioners could own and that customers would love, carrying the new purpose, Ignite Joy, into every line the company would write.

The strategy: purpose, belief, promise, pledges, personality. A launchpad for beautiful things.

The strategy surfaced what had been true since the beginning but had never been said out loud: Benefit exists to ignite joy, because beauty should lift people up, not bring them down. Everything followed from there — a promise of beauty made radically feel-good, and a personality that was exuberant, bold, fun, and unexpected. When it came to defining a cosmetics brand anchored in beauty that never took itself too seriously, it was clear that Benefit had been born that way — ever since the day a San Francisco exotic dancer walked into the Fords' original shop, The Face Place, and asked for a stain to tint her nipples. The bottle they mixed for her became Benetint.

The Fun-Loving Beauty Genius — the voice character that anchors every writing decision.

The verbal identity had to bridge that strategy and the page. Benefit knew more about brows than almost anyone alive and had never worked out how to sound like it — the copy swung between generic beauty-speak and irreverence cranked so high it never landed. So the voice had to solve one genuinely hard thing: how to sound like the most knowledgeable person in the beauty aisle and the most fun one, at the same time.

That person got a name: the Fun-Loving Beauty Genius — the friend who has tried everything in the aisle, obsessed over all of it, and can't wait to tell you what actually works. Think of the persona like a chic little hat a writer can wear: something you put on to get into character and take off when you're done. Three principles carried that character onto the page: talk to people like we've known them for ages, enthusiastically share what we know, and keep it playful.

Three voice principles. Specific enough to change how a writer tackles a TikTok script. Flexible enough   to work across fifty countries.

The principles worked as a system. Which one leads depends on the job in front of you: a product description, a crisis reply, and a welcome email don't ask for the same thing. A framework called Keeping Things Flexible mapped the three principles across five communication goals — informing, supporting, selling, entertaining, and honoring the moment — giving writers a useful toolkit that helps the brand voice travel, adapt, and connect to what audiences are feeling in any situation.

Before and after. Smarter, warmer, and finally the same brand talking every time.

The before-and-afters were where the voice went from theory to practice. Set side by side across product pages, headlines, TikTok scripts, and Instagram captions, they let writers see the moves in action. "Love your brows" becomes "Your brows. Our obsession." "Welcome to Benefit" becomes "You made it! We're so glad you've joined the Benefam." A product description that once read like a lab report becomes a brief, absurd vacation with Gimme Brow+. The rewrites are smarter and warmer, and their wit now has a specific role to play. And for the first time, the brand was built to sound like the same person across every surface.

The voice spectrum in action. Social, product, TikTok, site copy — each context, same character.

Then the system had to travel the world. Benefit writes in more than fifty countries — including English, Mandarin, Italian, and French — so the guidelines were built to inspire rather than dictate: explicit about intent, and honest about the limits of any rulebook written in one language for people working in another. We ran workshops with the global writing teams to find where a principle crashed and burned in another language before a market did.

A set of guardrails codified the brand's own vernacular — inclusive address, feel-good language, premium language, and the house rules of what Benefit calls Bene-fication. Each one protected the brand's warmth from its own worst instincts, keeping a close eye on the line between a brand that waves everyone into the beauty locker room and one that accidentally locks half of them out.


Voice in the wild. The new voice at work across campaign, social, and in-store touchpoints.

The voice became the foundation for everything Benefit wrote, and the blueprint every partner agency built from. A brand that had spent fifty years being felt — in the products, the pink packaging, the brow bars — finally had something to say.

Freshfields

ClientFreshfields AgencyWolff Olins RoleStrategy & Verbal Lead ScopeBrand strategy, naming, verbal identity

The new Freshfields wordmark: a single name in a bold uppercase, for a firm born to lead law forward.

By 2023, global law was being run from New York. American firms had the scale to win everywhere and the leverage to hire anyone. London’s old guard faced a choice: merge with a US firm as an insurance policy, or compete on American terms without becoming American. Most took the merger. Freshfields — 280 years old, 17 countries, one of the five magic circle firms — had just elected a leadership team with the opposite mandate: show up as one firm and meet the Americans on their home turf. One partner put it more plainly: “They’re eating our lunch, so we’re going to eat their dinner.”

My work spanned two places where that mandate had to hit home: the strategy defining what the brand stands for, and the language carrying it into the world. Both started with the name. The firm had operated for years as Freshfields Bruckhaus Deringer — a post-merger mutant carrying former firms, former allegiances, and decades of entrenched institutional politics. Our first recommendation was the most consequential: drop “Bruckhaus Deringer” and let the firm show up everywhere as one word.

That word first had to be won internally. Partners had spent whole careers identifying with the compound name, and the case for cutting it had to be airtight: the name they were protecting was costing them the unity it was designed to represent. We built the case for change and drove the campaign across the firm, Frankfurt to San Francisco. The firm went to market as Freshfields.

The brand platform: forefront speaks to constant innovation; edge echoes the client benefit. 

The strategic question came next: what could Freshfields claim that clients would believe and competitors couldn’t? Law firms tend to converge on the same superlatives. The research pointed somewhere more interesting. Freshfields is a genuine pioneer in law — the world’s most innovative companies bring it their biggest commercial leaps, industry stalwarts trust it to steady the ship through regulatory instability, and the firm hires leaders who can toggle between imaginative problem-solving and technical precision. Forward-thinking and technically unimpeachable at once, which gives clients the confidence to make bold decisions. So we anchored the Freshfields at the forefront of law, giving clients the edge.

The verbal identity: messaging and tone inspired by the feeling of a frank conversation with a Freshfields partner.

The verbal identity gave that positioning a voice, modeled on the feeling of a  frank conversation with a senior Freshfields partner. That partner leads with a strong point of view, is willing to tell you the thing you’d rather not hear, and backs it up with the precision of someone who has spent years inside the most complex deals in the world. The messaging frameworks, tone principles, and writing guidelines were built to reproduce that feeling in every piece of communication the firm makes.

The new brand in market: the same frank, forward-thinking voice across website, communications, and campaign.

Forefront and edge, carried across the firm’s real-world touchpoints.

The cultural dimension mattered as much as the strategy. American law-firm culture runs on “eat what you kill.” Freshfields had always been rooted in a British legal culture that colleagues and clients describe as genuinely collaborative — and that was an asset worth protecting even as the firm pushed onto American turf. Competing on US terms forced a real concession: Freshfields broke its lockstep pay structure for the first time in its history. Keeping the culture became the counterweight — a talent-market position as the firm where you can win at the highest level and be treated like a human being while you do.

The transformation was endorsed at the top and socialized across 420-plus partners, 2,800 lawyers, and 4,700 employees in 17 countries, and the market answered fast — US revenues rose from £391 million to £473 million in a year. The cleanest verdict came from the other side of the table, a rival New York dealmaker talking to the FT: “Other UK firms have tried to do what Freshfields is doing and they all failed.” Dinner, served.

On Acid: A Field Guide to Altered States

On Acid: A Field Guide to Altered States — John Moeller & William Rauscher, 2011.

On Acid: A Field Guide to Altered States is a 2011 experimental softcover publication by John Moeller and William Rauscher that explores humanity's engagement with altered states of consciousness. Tracing a historical line from Benjamin Blood's 1874 nitrous oxide writings through figures like William James and Timothy Leary to modern art, the book features contributions and archival texts from notable thinkers and artists including Albert Hofmann, Aldous Huxley, and Alan Watts.

Disentangled from familiar cultural contexts, On Acid is an experimental program, an acidic editorial process that mirrors the deconstructive relations to counterculture cultivated in contemporary art. The book concludes with a series of new conversations with Freeman and Lowe, Hamilton Morris, and Arik Roper.

Earthseed Design

Github ↗

My artistic practice is powered by collisions between technology and literature. I treat literature as a language system and apply intelligent tools that open up new layers of meaning and scramble the divides between author, work and audience. 

For the Earthseed Design System, I applied the form and logic of the markdown file to create a machine-readable instruction manual based on the narrative worlds of Octavia Butler's speculative fictional novels, Parable of the Sower and Parable of the Talents. In the Parable novels Butler crafted a religion — Earthseed, whose one certainty is that God is Change — and left it as scripture and instruction. Earthseed Design asks a designer's question of it: if a belief had to spread across broken, scattered communities with no head office and no budget, what would its design system have to be?

The answer is the Earthseed Design System. I used an LLM agent to scan the entire text of the first Parable novel and extract the rules and principles that would allow anyone to build their own Earthseed world—including founding their own community, just as the novel's protagonist does. The design system provides the tools that keep Earthseed recognizable from one community to the next: a creative position, doctrine read as a design constraint, and specifications for color and typography. It is written for people with no access to industrial production, built for portability and survival. There is no license and no central authority — anyone holding the document already has permission to use it.

Like a Sol LeWitt certificate, the document is the work: a set of instructions written once and executed by many hands. For example, you can use it to design a logo, a religious pamphlet, a water barrel for your Earthseed community, or an approach to your community's funeral rites. The Earthseed Design System also fulfills one of the missions native to the Parable story: to keep spreading knowledge and practice of Earthseed.

Earthseed Design System is made for a world that has to improvise — and the clearest way I know to show what a design system is actually for: the smallest shared language that lets a thing stay recognizably itself while every hand that touches it makes it their own.
